## Universal Evaluation Dimensions

Survey reviewers usually assess four core dimensions.

### 1. Scope and Coverage

**What reviewers ask:**
- Is the scope clearly defined?
- Does the survey cover the most important work in the area?
- Are the boundaries of the survey honest and explicit?

**How to improve scope and coverage:**
- State the survey scope in one sentence.
- Explain what is included and excluded.
- Justify any omissions.
- Add missing foundational or representative papers.

### 2. Organization and Synthesis

**What reviewers ask:**
- Is the literature organized by a useful taxonomy or theme?
- Does the paper explain relationships between papers instead of listing them?
- Does the structure help readers understand the field?

**How to improve organization and synthesis:**
- Use a taxonomy, timeline, or theme-based structure.
- Compare methods, assumptions, and tradeoffs.
- Highlight recurring patterns and disagreements.
- Use summary tables where they reduce repetition.

### 3. Accuracy and Evidence

**What reviewers ask:**
- Are claims about prior work accurate?
- Are citations correct and current?
- Are important claims supported by the cited papers?

**How to improve accuracy and evidence:**
- Verify every citation from a source record or LightRAG result.
- Prefer primary sources over secondary summaries.
- Avoid unsupported generalizations.
- Keep factual statements tightly coupled to citations.

### 4. Usefulness and Clarity

**What reviewers ask:**
- Does the survey help readers navigate the area?
- Is the writing clear and accessible to the intended audience?
- Does the paper identify open problems or next steps?

**How to improve usefulness and clarity:**
- Write concise section introductions and transitions.
- Explain why the survey is useful.
- Add a clear open-problems section.
- Remove unnecessary repetition.

## What Makes a Strong Survey Review

### Strong Review Structure

A useful review should include:

1. A short summary of the survey's scope and contribution.
2. Strengths of the organization and synthesis.
3. Gaps in coverage or framing.
4. Specific suggestions for improving the taxonomy, comparison, or completeness.
5. Clear feedback on whether the survey helps readers understand the field.

### Review Style Principles

- Re-express the survey fairly before critiquing it.
- Focus on evidence and organization, not personal preference.
- Distinguish missing coverage from weak synthesis.
- Prioritize issues that affect the reader's understanding.

## Common Survey Concerns

| Issue | What it Means | Fix |
|------|---------------|-----|
| Narrow scope | The survey leaves out an important sub-area | Expand the corpus or explain the boundary |
| Paper-by-paper listing | The draft summarizes items without synthesis | Reorganize by theme, taxonomy, or comparison axis |
| Weak novelty of organization | The structure is obvious or unhelpful | Introduce a more informative organizing principle |
| Citation gaps | Key papers are missing | Add the missing papers and revise the taxonomy |
| Unsupported claims | A statement is not tied to a source | Add a citation or remove the claim |
| Vague open problems | The future work section is generic | Name concrete unresolved questions |

## How to Revise a Survey Draft

1. Tighten the scope statement.
2. Rebuild the taxonomy if needed.
3. Add missing papers and citations.
4. Compare methods on dimensions that matter to readers.
5. Replace repeated summaries with synthesis.
6. Strengthen the open problems and conclusion.

### Revision Checklist

- [ ] The scope is explicit.
- [ ] The taxonomy is useful.
- [ ] The corpus is complete enough for the stated scope.
- [ ] Every key claim is cited.
- [ ] The paper explains what readers learn from the survey.
- [ ] The conclusion restates the contribution in different words.
